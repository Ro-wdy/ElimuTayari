# M-STA-02: Probability 1

**Inquiry question:** How is probability applied in real-life situations?

## Curriculum alignment

The design covers experiments involving probability, the range of probability, probability
spaces, mutually exclusive and independent events, the addition and multiplication laws,
tree diagrams, and real-life application. The source design indicates 12 lessons.

**Concepts:** experiment, outcome, sample space, event, equally likely, experimental

## Teacher pack

### Learning sequence

1. Run coin, dice, or spinner experiments. Record all outcomes, repeat enough trials, and
   compare experimental frequency with a theoretical expectation.
2. Define probability on the range `0 <= P(A) <= 1`; 0 means impossible and 1 means certain.
   Build the sample space before calculating.
3. Separate mutually exclusive events (cannot happen together) from independent events (one
   does not change the chance of the other). Use `P(A or B)=P(A)+P(B)` only when disjoint;
   use `P(A and B)=P(A)P(B)` for independent events.
4. Draw tree diagrams for successive independent or conditional stages. Multiply along a path
   and add disjoint paths. Label assumptions and check that all terminal probabilities sum to 1.

### Low-resource activity

Groups toss a coin 30 times and record heads/tails. Compare their experimental probability
with 1/2. Then draw a two-toss tree and explain why the four equally likely outcomes appear.

### Kenyan context

Use fair-game design, weather or transport scenarios, or the source design's road-safety
discussion. Probability can inform decisions; it does not guarantee an individual outcome.

### Check for understanding

- List a complete sample space for a simple experiment.
- Classify pairs of events as mutually exclusive, independent, both, or neither.
- Select addition versus multiplication law and justify the choice.
- Complete a tree diagram and verify terminal probabilities sum to 1.

### Common misconceptions

- Treating probability as a percentage without converting consistently.
- Adding probabilities for events that overlap.
- Calling events independent because they are different; independence is about influence.
- Assuming a short experiment must match theory exactly (the law of large numbers is not a
  guarantee for a small sample).

## Worked example

For two fair coin tosses, the sample space is `HH, HT, TH, TT`. The probability of exactly one
head is `P(HT)+P(TH)=1/4+1/4=1/2`. The events are disjoint paths, so addition is appropriate.
