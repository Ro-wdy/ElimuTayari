# M-GEO-07: Surface Area and Volume of Solids

**Inquiry questions:** How do we determine the surface area and volume of solids? Why do we
determine them?

## Curriculum alignment

Learners determine surface area and volume for prisms, pyramids, cones, frustums, and spheres;
find surface area and volume of composite solids; and explore real-life uses. The design
explicitly encourages models, nets, and a cone/cylinder comparison showing that a cone with
the same base and height has one third of the cylinder's volume.

**Concepts:** net, curved surface, total surface area, prism, pyramid, cone, frustum, sphere,
hemisphere, composite solid, cross-section, volume, capacity.

**CBC emphasis:** critical thinking and problem solving, creativity and imagination; unity
and respect; environment and technology.

## Teacher pack

### Learning sequence

1. Distinguish surface area (square units, exposed faces) from volume (cubic units, space
   occupied). Unfold prisms and pyramids into nets.
2. Build formulas from familiar bases and heights: prism `V=area of base x length`, pyramid
   `V=1/3 x area of base x perpendicular height`, cone `V=1/3 pi r^2 h`, sphere
   `V=4/3 pi r^3`.
3. Add exposed faces for composite solids only once. For curved solids, identify slant height
   versus perpendicular height before choosing a surface formula.
4. Use sand or water with same-base, same-height cone and cylinder models to test the one-third
   relationship, then apply formulas to containers and packaging.

### Low-resource activity

Make nets from old cartons. Label every face, fold the solid, calculate surface area, then
fill a matching container with measured cups to estimate volume and compare with the formula.

### Kenyan context

Estimate paint for a cylindrical water tank or cardboard for a carton, and compare storage
volumes. Ask which surfaces are actually exposed before pricing material.

### Check for understanding

- Select a formula from the solid's base, height, and slant data.
- Explain why pyramid/cone volume includes `1/3`.
- Separate exposed and hidden faces in a composite solid.
- Convert between cubic units and litres only after calculating volume.

### Common misconceptions

- Confusing surface area with volume or mixing square and cubic units.
- Using slant height as perpendicular height in volume.
- Counting an internal joining face in the exposed surface area.
- Applying `1/3` to a prism or cylinder volume.

## Worked example

A cone has radius 3 cm and perpendicular height 8 cm. Its volume is
`V=1/3 x pi x 3^2 x 8 = 24pi cm^3`, approximately `75.4 cm^3`. The 8 cm height is the
perpendicular height, not the slant edge.

## SMS teaching pack

**SMS 1/3:** `M-GEO-07` Surface area uses square units; volume uses cubic units. Prism V=base area x length.

**SMS 2/3:** Pyramid/cone V=1/3 x base area x perpendicular height. Cone and cylinder with same base/height: cone is one third.
